You step out of the shuttle into Antigua's thin morning light. The cobbles are uneven underfoot, a church façade glows the colour of egg yolk, and above the rooftops a volcano wears a collar of cloud. Somewhere a bell is ringing; somewhere else a woman is patting tortillas between her palms, a soft, steady clap that sounds like applause for the day. You have only just arrived, and already Guatemala feels like a country with more in it than one trip can hold.

That is the first thing to know: it does have more than one trip can hold. The second is that it fits together more simply than it first appears. This guide walks through the shape of the country, when to go, how to move around, and how to travel well, so you can plan your first visit with confidence.

## How the country fits together

Most first trips trace a loop through four areas. Think of them as chapters rather than stops.

### Antigua

The old colonial capital is where most travellers begin, and for good reason. It is compact, walkable and ringed by volcanoes, with ruined convents, courtyards hung with bougainvillea and a gentle rhythm that eases you into the country. It is also the base for the overnight volcano hikes many people come for. Our guide to [what to see in Antigua and how long to stay](/posts/antigua-what-to-see-how-long-to-stay/) goes deeper.

### The highlands and Lake Atitlán

West of Antigua the land climbs into the highlands: pine-covered ridges, terraced maize fields and market towns where Maya life sets the pace. Lake Atitlán sits in a basin among volcanoes, its shore dotted with villages that each have a distinct character, linked by small boats. Chichicastenango's market is often folded into this part of the trip. Choosing where to sleep around the lake is half the fun; we compare the options in [which Lake Atitlán village to stay in](/posts/lake-atitlan-which-village-to-stay-in/).

### Petén and Tikal

In the far north, the lowland forest of Petén holds Tikal, the great Maya city whose temples rise above the canopy. Most travellers base themselves in Flores, a small island town on a lake, and reach the region by domestic flight from Guatemala City rather than by the long road north.

### Alta Verapaz and Semuc Champey

Between the highlands and Petén lies Alta Verapaz, a green, misty region of cloud forest and coffee. Its best-known sight, Semuc Champey, is a run of turquoise limestone pools above a river. Getting there is slow and winding, which is exactly why some travellers love it and others skip it.

Trying to see all four in a week is a recipe for spending your holiday in a vehicle. With around ten days, Antigua, Atitlán and Tikal make a comfortable first loop; add Alta Verapaz if you have longer. Our [ten-day Guatemala itinerary](/posts/ten-day-guatemala-itinerary/) shows one way to string them together.

## When to go: dry season and rainy season

Guatemala has two broad seasons rather than four. The UK Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office says the rainy season [normally runs from May to November](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/guatemala/safety-and-security), overlapping with the Caribbean hurricane season, and that heavy rain can cause flooding and landslides that disrupt travel routes (UK FCDO travel advice, checked November 2026).

The drier months outside that window bring clearer skies, easier roads and the best chance of volcano views, and they are also the busiest. The rainy season has its own appeal: greener hills, quieter towns and mornings that are often bright before afternoon showers roll in. If you travel then, build some slack into your plans and keep an eye on local conditions.

Altitude matters as much as season. Antigua and the highlands can feel cool, especially at night, while Petén is hot and humid year-round. Pack layers.

## Getting around

For most first-timers, the backbone of the trip is the tourist shuttle: shared minibuses that run between the main destinations and pick up from hotels. INGUAT's tourist assistance service notes that you can hire group transport or shuttles to places such as Antigua and Lake Atitlán ([INGUAT Tourist Assistance recommendations](https://asistur.gt/en/recomendaciones/), checked November 2026). Private transfers cost more but give you flexibility and door-to-door comfort.

The brightly painted former school buses are a vivid sight, but the UK FCDO advises travellers to [avoid public buses](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/guatemala/safety-and-security) because of safety risks and frequent accidents, and INGUAT recommends travelling to the interior during the day and on main roads. In towns, use authorised, clearly identified taxis or a ride booked through your hotel.

For Petén, a domestic flight to Flores turns a long journey into a short hop. Book it early in your planning, since it tends to shape the rest of the itinerary. Around Lake Atitlán, boats are how you move between villages.

## Money and cards

The currency is the quetzal. Cards are accepted at many hotels, restaurants and larger shops, and INGUAT says it is safe to use credit cards in Guatemala, while advising that you split your money and documents rather than keeping everything in one place ([INGUAT Tourist Assistance recommendations](https://asistur.gt/en/recomendaciones/)). Markets, small cafés, tuk-tuks and village boats usually want cash, so carry a modest amount in small notes.

The UK FCDO notes that British pounds are often hard to exchange, while US dollars are much easier, and advises checking ATMs for signs of tampering and changing money at banks, hotels or foreign exchange offices ([UK FCDO travel advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/guatemala/safety-and-security), checked November 2026). Tell your bank you are travelling, and use ATMs inside banks or shopping centres during the day.

## Language

Spanish is the language you will use for most practical things, and even a few phrases go a long way. But Guatemala is also home to many Maya languages, and in much of the highlands a Maya language such as K'iche', Kaqchikel or Tz'utujil is spoken at home and in the market. Learning to say hello or thank you in the local language of a place you are staying is a small courtesy that is warmly received. Antigua and the lake are also popular places to take Spanish classes, if you want to linger.

## Entry requirements

Rules depend on your nationality, so start with the [Instituto Guatemalteco de Migración](https://igm.gob.gt/), Guatemala's immigration authority, and your own government's advice.

For British citizens, the UK FCDO says your passport must be valid for [at least 6 months after the date you arrive](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/guatemala/entry-requirements) and have at least 2 blank pages, and that you can visit for up to 90 days without a visa. It also says you must complete an online immigration declaration form before you enter or exit, and that you should make sure your passport is stamped on arrival (UK FCDO entry requirements, checked November 2026).

If you plan to continue to El Salvador, Honduras or Nicaragua, note that the FCDO says the same 90-day period covers travel between all four countries and does not restart when you cross between them. US travellers should check the State Department's [Guatemala page on travel.state.gov](https://travel.state.gov/content/tsg_aem/us/en/home/international-travel/travel-advisories/destination.gtm.html). Entry rules change, so re-check them shortly before you fly.

## Health

See a travel health professional well before your trip. The UK FCDO's [health page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/guatemala/health) suggests doing so at least 8 weeks ahead, flags altitude sickness as a risk in parts of the country, and notes that British prescriptions cannot be used in Guatemala, so bring what you need (UK FCDO travel advice, checked November 2026).

The [CDC's Guatemala page](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/destinations/traveler/none/guatemala) recommends hepatitis A and typhoid vaccines for most travellers and routine vaccines including measles. It says there is no malaria transmission in Antigua, Guatemala City or at Lake Atitlán, but recommends preventive medication for certain other areas, so ask your doctor if you are heading to Petén or rural lowlands. Mosquito-borne illnesses such as dengue are present, so pack repellent (CDC, checked November 2026). Drink bottled or treated water, and take out travel insurance that covers medical treatment and evacuation.

## What you'll eat

Food in Guatemala starts with maize. Tortillas arrive warm, wrapped in a cloth, with almost every meal, thicker and softer than you may be used to, with a faint smoky sweetness from the griddle. Breakfast is often eggs, black beans, fried plantain, fresh cheese and cream, with a cup of local coffee.

Then there are the stews. Pepián is the one people talk about: a rich, reddish-brown sauce built from roasted tomatoes, chillies and toasted seeds, with chicken or beef and vegetables, deeply savoury and warming on a cool highland evening. Look out too for jocón, a green stew of tomatillos and herbs, and kak'ik, a spiced turkey soup from Alta Verapaz. Tamales, wrapped in leaves and steamed, appear in many forms, and on the lake you may find fish from its waters.

Street snacks are part of the pleasure: chuchitos, small tamales; tostadas piled with guacamole or beans; and, in the evenings, rellenitos, sweet plantain filled with black beans. Eat where the turnover is high and the food is cooked in front of you.

## Travelling with respect

The Maya communities you will meet are living cultures, not a backdrop. Dress modestly in villages and churches, greet people before you start a conversation or a purchase, and bargain lightly and with good humour in markets.

Always ask before photographing people, and accept a no. The UK FCDO is especially firm on this: it advises [never taking photographs without permission](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/guatemala/safety-and-security), particularly of children, and notes that it is a legal requirement to carry ID (UK FCDO travel advice, checked November 2026). Ceremonies in churches and at sacred sites are acts of faith; watch quietly from the side, or not at all if that seems right.

## Staying safe

Both the UK and US governments advise caution in Guatemala, and some border areas carry specific warnings. Most visitors to the main routes travel without incident, but it pays to plan sensibly. Our guide [is Guatemala safe for travellers?](/posts/is-guatemala-safe-for-travellers/) sets out exactly what the official advice says today. Check the [UK FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/guatemala) and US State Department advice again just before you travel.

A first trip to Guatemala rewards a little structure: the right order of places, transfers that line up, a guide who knows when Tikal is quietest. A local operator can stitch those pieces together so you spend your days on the cobbles, the water and the temple steps rather than on logistics, and have someone to call if plans change along the way.
