The church bells start before the light does. You lie in a room with thick white walls and listen to the town wake up: a broom on cobblestones, a bus horn somewhere beyond the arch, the clatter of a café shutter. When you step outside, Volcán de Agua is already there at the end of the street, green and enormous, wearing a scarf of cloud. You have ten days, and the whole country seems to be waiting just past that volcano.

Ten days is a good length for a first trip to Guatemala. It is long enough to see the four places most people come for, Antigua, Lake Atitlán, the highland market at Chichicastenango and the Maya city of Tikal, without spending every other day in a vehicle. It is not long enough to see everything, and the plan below is honest about that. Treat it as a shape to adapt, not a timetable to obey.

## The shape of the trip

The route makes a loop through the highlands and then a jump north. You start in Antigua, close to the international airport, and settle in. You head west and up into the hills to Chichicastenango and down to Lake Atitlán. Then you come back through Guatemala City and take a domestic flight north to Flores, the island town that is the usual base for Tikal. You fly back to the capital at the end.

That one flight is what makes ten days work. The road between the highlands and the Petén is very long, most of a day or more, and flying turns it into a morning. Book it early, and keep the day of your international departure free of anything that could make you miss it.

## Day by day

### Day 1–3: Antigua

Arrive, transfer to Antigua and do very little. The town sits high in a valley ringed by volcanoes, and the first evening is best spent wandering: the central park as the light goes, the yellow arch, the ruined churches and convents that earthquakes left standing in pieces. Antigua rewards slow walking more than any checklist, which is why it is worth at least two full days.

On day two, take a walking tour with a local guide to understand the colonial history and the earthquakes that shaped it, then give the afternoon to markets, galleries and coffee. On day three, head into the hills to a coffee farm, or simply keep exploring at your own pace.

**The Acatenango option.** If you are fit and want the most dramatic night of the trip, swap days two and three for the overnight hike up Acatenango. You climb through farmland and cloud forest to a high camp, and after dark, if neighbouring Fuego is active, you watch it throw up fire. It is hard work, cold at night, and needs a guide. The UK Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office notes that some volcanoes are at high altitude with freezing temperatures at night, that there is no mountain rescue service, and advises checking the websites of [INSIVUMEH](https://www.insivumeh.gob.gt/), Guatemala's meteorological and volcanology institute, and [CONRED](https://conred.gob.gt/), its disaster agency, for access, restrictions and recommendations before you climb ([UK FCDO travel advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/guatemala/safety-and-security), checked November 2026). Re-check both the week you go. Our piece on [hiking Acatenango to watch Fuego erupt](/posts/hiking-acatenango-to-watch-fuego-erupt/) covers what the climb asks of you. Allow a quiet day afterwards; your legs will want it.

### Day 4: Chichicastenango on market day, then on to the lake

This is the day to plan the rest of the trip around. Chichicastenango's traditional market takes place on Thursdays and Sundays ([Visit Centroamérica](https://www.visitcentroamerica.com/en/visitar/santo-tomas-church-pascual-abaj-shrine-and-chichicastenango-market/), checked November 2026), so count backwards from those days when you fix your arrival date. If the calendar will not cooperate, you can visit as a day trip from the lake instead.

Leave Antigua early. By mid-morning the streets of Chichicastenango are lined with stalls: handwoven textiles, masks, pottery, flowers, fruit, and the everyday business of a market that exists for the people who live here, not for visitors. On the steps of the church of Santo Tomás, incense smoke drifts over the crowd. Remember that this is a working market and a place of worship in a living K'iche' Maya community. Ask before you photograph anyone, accept a no gracefully, and be especially careful around ceremonies. Our [Chichicastenango market day](/posts/chichicastenango-market-day/) guide has more on how to visit well.

In the afternoon, continue down to Lake Atitlán and arrive in time to watch the light change on the volcanoes across the water.

### Day 5–6: Lake Atitlán

Two full days at the lake is the minimum that feels unhurried. The villages around the shore each have their own character, and the easy way between them is by boat. Spend one day crossing the water between two or three villages, perhaps one for its market, one for its weaving, and a quieter one for lunch. Spend the other doing as little as possible. Mornings are usually calmest on the water, so if you want to kayak or swim, go early.

Choosing where to sleep matters more here than anywhere else on the route, because the villages are so different from each other: some lively, some almost silent after dark.

A note on roads: the UK FCDO passes on advice from PROATUR, Guatemala's tourist assistance service, to use the Pan-American Highway rather than the Godínez bypass between Guatemala City and Panajachel, and says boat services between lakeshore towns may be a safer alternative to some roads ([UK FCDO regional risks](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/guatemala/regional-risks), checked November 2026). A reputable driver will know this; it is still worth asking.

### Day 7: Fly north to the Petén

This is the longest travel day. Leave the lake in the morning, drive back to Guatemala City and take the domestic flight to Flores. By evening you are in a different Guatemala: low, hot, green, with the island's narrow streets reflected in Lake Petén Itzá. Have dinner by the water and go to bed early.

### Day 8–9: Tikal and the Petén

Go to Tikal as early as you can. The site lies deep in rainforest, and in the first hours of the day the howler monkeys are loud, the plazas are quiet and the temple tops rise out of the mist. Hire a licensed guide to make sense of the ruins and to spot the wildlife you would otherwise walk past. Our guide to [visiting Tikal at sunrise](/posts/visiting-tikal-at-sunrise/) explains the options, including staying near the park.

Use day nine for a second site in the Petén, a slower morning in Flores, or a boat trip on the lake. If you stayed near Tikal, you can make a second, quieter visit.

### Day 10: Home

Fly back to Guatemala City and connect with your international flight. Leave a generous gap between the two.

## If you have more time: add Semuc Champey

With three or four extra days, the obvious addition is Semuc Champey, a chain of turquoise limestone pools on top of a natural bridge, deep in the hills of Alta Verapaz. It is lovely and it is remote: the journey in is long and winding, and the last stretch is rough. Fit it between the highlands and the Petén, so you travel overland from one to the other and fly out from Flores. Read [whether Semuc Champey is worth the journey](/posts/semuc-champey-is-it-worth-the-journey/) before committing.

More time could also simply mean longer stays. An extra night in Antigua or at the lake costs nothing in travel and is often the best use of a spare day.

## If you have less time

With a week, drop something rather than squeezing everything. The cleanest cut is to choose between the highlands and Tikal. A week of Antigua, Chichicastenango and the lake is a complete, satisfying trip. So is Antigua plus the Petén, with the flight north. Trying to do all four in seven days means a travel day for every day of sightseeing, and you will remember the vehicles more than the places.

If you must do everything, skip Acatenango, keep Antigua to two nights and give the lake two nights rather than three.

## Pacing advice

- **Build in slack.** Roads in the highlands are slow and winding, and weather, roadworks or a market-day crowd can add hours. Do not schedule anything important on the afternoon of a long drive.
- **Stay put in threes.** Two-night stops feel rushed by the end of the trip. Where you can, give each base three nights.
- **Go early.** Markets, the lake and Tikal are all at their best in the first hours of the day.
- **Rest after Acatenango.** Plan an easy day afterwards rather than an early departure.
- **Protect the last day.** Nothing should stand between you and your flight home.

## When to go

The UK FCDO says Guatemala's rainy season normally runs from May to November, the same time as the Caribbean hurricane season, and that heavy rains can cause flooding and landslides ([UK FCDO travel advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/guatemala/safety-and-security), checked November 2026). Outside that period roads are generally more reliable and volcano views clearer. The rainy season is not a reason to stay away, as mornings are often bright and the landscape is at its greenest, but it is a reason to leave more slack in the plan and to check conditions as you go. Always re-check official advice before you travel.
