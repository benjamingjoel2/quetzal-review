You are standing in Antigua's central park as the evening cools. Families share ice creams on the benches, a marimba plays under the arches, and a pair of tourist police officers stroll past and nod. Across the square, someone is checking a phone for tomorrow's shuttle time to the lake. It feels relaxed, almost sleepy. And yet, before you booked, you probably read a government warning that gave you pause.

Both pictures are true. Guatemala is visited every year by travellers who have wonderful, trouble-free trips, and it is also a country where official advice is cautious for good reasons. The honest answer to "is it safe?" is that it depends on where you go, how you travel and what precautions you take. Here is what the UK and US governments actually say today, and what it means for a trip.

## What the official advisories say

### UK Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office

The [UK FCDO's travel advice for Guatemala](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/guatemala) carries this headline warning: "FCDO advises against all but essential travel to parts of Guatemala." The page was still current on 11 November 2026 and last updated on 5 November 2026 (UK FCDO travel advice, checked November 2026).

The areas it advises against all but essential travel to are:

- "within 5km of the Mexican border from the Pacific Coast up to and including the Gracias a Dios crossing"
- "the towns of Santa Ana Huista, San Antonio Huista and La Democracia in the department of Huehuetenango"

The FCDO does not currently advise against all travel to any part of the country. It points out that your travel insurance could be invalidated if you travel against its advice.

### US Department of State

The US State Department's [travel advisory for Guatemala](https://travel.state.gov/content/tsg_aem/us/en/home/international-travel/travel-advisories/destination.gtm.html) is "Level 3: Reconsider Travel", with the summary: "Reconsider travel to Guatemala due to crime and terrorism. Some areas have higher risks." The State Department's travel advisories feed dates this entry 12 March 2026 (checked November 2026).

It lists these "Do not travel" areas, each "due to crime and terrorism by cartels, gangs, and criminal organizations":

- San Marcos Department
- Huehuetenango Department
- Zone 18 in Guatemala City and the city of Villa Nueva

The same advisory notes that US government employees may visit popular destinations such as Tikal, Antigua and Lake Atitlán, but should avoid driving outside Guatemala City at night, and may not use white taxis or public "chicken buses".

None of the classic first-time stops (Antigua, Lake Atitlán, Chichicastenango, Tikal and Flores, Semuc Champey) sit in the zones either government tells you to avoid. The FCDO does, though, note high levels of crime in and around several departments, including Petén, and advises staying within tourist areas and travelling in daylight where possible. Read the advice in full, and re-check both pages shortly before you travel: they can change at short notice.

## What the risks actually are

The FCDO is blunt that Guatemala has a high level of violent crime, that most serious crime involves local gangs, and that incidents can happen in tourist areas. It says there have been armed attacks or robberies on most tourist routes between Guatemala City, Antigua, Tikal, Petén and Lake Atitlán, and it describes express kidnappings, often involving taxis hailed on the street, and criminals posing as police ([UK FCDO safety and security](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/guatemala/safety-and-security), checked November 2026). The US advisory adds that tourists can become victims of opportunistic crimes such as scams and extortion.

That is sobering reading. What it translates to on the ground is a set of habits rather than a reason to stay home:

- Keep phones, cameras and jewellery out of sight, and carry only the cash you need.
- Travel by day where you can, and on main roads.
- Use authorised, clearly identified taxis, a ride booked by your hotel, or a pre-booked transfer, never one flagged down on the street.
- If someone tries to rob you, hand things over. The FCDO notes that attackers have hurt victims who resisted.
- Tell your hotel where you are going, as INGUAT recommends ([INGUAT Tourist Assistance recommendations](https://asistur.gt/en/recomendaciones/), checked November 2026).

In Guatemala City, the FCDO says no area is free from crime, including the popular Zone 10, and advises care in Zone 1, the historic centre. Many travellers simply transfer from the airport straight to Antigua.

## Road travel and shuttles

Getting between places is where most of your planning should go. The FCDO advises [avoiding public buses](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/guatemala/safety-and-security), the repainted US school buses, because of gang attacks on drivers, poor maintenance and frequent, sometimes fatal, accidents. It says private intercity coaches are generally safer, though not immune. Most visitors use tourist shuttles or private transfers, and INGUAT mentions shuttles as an option to places such as Antigua and Lake Atitlán.

Around Lake Atitlán, the FCDO passes on route advice from Guatemala's tourist assistance service: avoid the Godínez bypass through Patzún between Guatemala City and Panajachel, using the Pan-American Highway to Sololá instead, and avoid the road between Cocales and San Lucas Tolimán if possible. It notes that boats between lakeside towns may be a safer alternative ([UK FCDO regional risks](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/guatemala/regional-risks), checked November 2026).

If you drive, the FCDO advises sticking to main roads, travelling in convoy where possible, and being ready for police checkpoints. PROVIAL, the roadside assistance service, can be reached on +502 2419 2121 or 1520, though the FCDO says patrols are infrequent.

## Volcanoes, earthquakes and the rainy season

Guatemala's volcanoes are a big reason people come, and they are active. Before any climb, the FCDO advises checking [INSIVUMEH](https://insivumeh.gob.gt/), the national institute for volcanology, seismology and meteorology, which publishes volcano bulletins, and [CONRED](https://conred.gob.gt/), the national disaster-reduction agency, for access, restrictions and recommendations. Follow the advice of local authorities.

The FCDO also warns that some volcanoes are at high altitude with freezing nights, that warm clothing and waterproofs are essential, that local tour organisers tend to underestimate the risks, that deaths have occurred, and that there is no mountain rescue service. Earth tremors are frequent, and the rainy season, which it says normally runs from May to November, brings flooding and landslides that can close roads ([UK FCDO safety and security](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/guatemala/safety-and-security), checked November 2026). ## Hiking with a guide

INGUAT recommends using tourist guide services it has authorised when visiting volcanoes and natural or archaeological parks, sticking to established trails, and travelling in a group to beaches, archaeological parks and nature reserves ([INGUAT Tourist Assistance recommendations](https://asistur.gt/en/recomendaciones/)). The tourist police division, DISETUR, lists accompaniment for the ascent and descent of volcanoes among its services ([INGUAT Tourist Assistance services](https://asistur.gt/en/servicios/), checked November 2026). In practice, that means no solo hikes on volcanoes or remote trails, and choosing a reputable outfitter over the cheapest offer.

## Money and ATMs

The FCDO advises checking ATMs for signs of tampering and says it is safer to change money at banks, hotels or foreign exchange offices. INGUAT says it is safe to use credit cards, advises not accepting help from strangers at ATMs, and recommends using only the banking system to change money. Use machines inside banks or shopping centres in daylight, withdraw modest amounts, and split cash and cards between bags.

## Solo and women travellers

Neither government publishes separate Guatemala-specific advice for women or solo travellers. The FCDO links from its Guatemala page to its general guidance for women travellers and for solo and independent travel, and its Guatemala advice says violent attacks, including sexual assault, can happen anywhere and at any time. It advises against travelling alone or at night, especially near borders or in quiet areas, and suggests travelling with others or a reputable tour company in remote areas. Plenty of people travel solo in Guatemala; the main routes, shared shuttles and group tours make it easier to have company when it matters.

## Photography and local customs

The FCDO asks travellers not to take photographs without permission, especially of children, and particularly in more remote areas, noting that foreigners have been caught up in violence linked to rumours of child abduction. Ask first, every time. It also says you must carry ID, and that in San Pedro La Laguna on Lake Atitlán, local authorities may not accept a photocopy of your passport.

## Who to call

Save these before you go. INGUAT's tourist assistance service, called PROATUR by the UK FCDO and ASISTUR by the US State Department, runs a 24-hour line in English and Spanish ([INGUAT Tourist Assistance](https://asistur.gt/en/recomendaciones/), checked November 2026):

- Tourist assistance: 1500, or +502 2290 2810
- Tourist assistance on WhatsApp: +502 5188 1819 (listed by INGUAT for escort requests and by the US State Department advisory)
- Tourist Security Division (DISETUR): +502 5551 5547
- National Civil Police: 110
- Volunteer firefighters: 122; municipal firefighters: 123
- Red Cross: 125

INGUAT also offers escorts for tourists through DISETUR, requested via an online form at least 72 hours in advance and subject to availability ([INGUAT Tourist Assistance services](https://asistur.gt/en/servicios/)). If you are the victim of a crime, the FCDO says PROATUR can accompany you to your hotel or another safe place.

## So, should you go?

For most travellers following the well-worn route, the answer is yes, with your eyes open. Read the advisories in full, plan your transfers before you land, travel by day, hike with authorised guides and keep a low profile with valuables.
