The smoke reaches you before the church does. Copal incense drifts in blue-white ribbons over the plaza, catching the low morning sun, and on the whitewashed steps of Santo Tomás a man swings a smoking can in slow arcs while murmuring prayers. Below him, the market is still waking up: tarpaulins being tied to poles, bundles of cloth unrolled in bursts of red and purple, a woman setting down a basket of flowers, the first tortillas slapping between palms. Someone calls a greeting in K'iche'. A dog weaves between the stalls. By mid-morning the lanes will be shoulder to shoulder, but for now you can hear the town breathe.

Chichicastenango, often shortened to Chichi, is the most famous market town in the Guatemalan highlands, and on market days it is one of the most vivid places in the country. It is also a living K'iche' Maya community where the church steps and the hilltop shrine are places of worship, not stage sets. Come curious, come early, and come ready to be a considerate guest.

## When to go: market days

The traditional market takes place on Thursdays and Sundays, according to Visit Centroamérica, the official regional tourism site for Central America ([Visit Centroamérica, checked November 2026](https://www.visitcentroamerica.com/en/visitar/santo-tomas-church-pascual-abaj-shrine-and-chichicastenango-market/)). On other days the town is much quieter, which has its own appeal, but the market itself is the reason most people come.

Sunday is often described as the busier of the two days, and Thursday can feel a little more relaxed. Either way, arriving in the morning gives you the best light, the most space to move and the chance to see the market being set up rather than packed away. Special occasions such as Holy Week or the town's patronal festival can change the rhythm, so check locally if your visit coincides with one.

## Who you are visiting

Chichicastenango sits in the department of El Quiché, and the Academia de Lenguas Mayas de Guatemala lists it among the municipalities of the K'iche' linguistic community ([ALMG Vocabulario de Sinónimos K'iche'](https://almg.org.gt/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Sinonimos-Kiche.pdf)). You will hear K'iche' spoken all around you in the market, alongside Spanish.

K'iche' life here weaves together Catholic and Maya spiritual practice. Visit Centroamérica describes the church of Santo Tomás as a place where "ancestral ceremonies and Catholicism are mixed", and notes that the church has several cofradías, the religious brotherhoods that care for the saints' images ([Visit Centroamérica](https://www.visitcentroamerica.com/en/visitar/santo-tomas-church-pascual-abaj-shrine-and-chichicastenango-market/)). What you see on the steps and at the shrine is prayer. Treat it that way.

## The church of Santo Tomás and its steps

The church stands at the edge of the market plaza, its steps often wreathed in incense and lined with people praying, selling flowers or simply sitting. Visit Centroamérica describes a staircase of 18 steps representing the months of the Maya calendar, and says this is where the Popol Vuh, the sacred book of the K'iche', was discovered ([Visit Centroamérica](https://www.visitcentroamerica.com/en/visitar/santo-tomas-church-pascual-abaj-shrine-and-chichicastenango-market/)).

A few points of etiquette make a real difference:

- **Use the side entrance.** The front steps and main door are commonly reserved for prayer and ritual. Visitors usually enter by a side door; ask locally or follow your guide.
- **Do not photograph inside the church**, and do not photograph people praying on the steps without clear permission. If in doubt, put the camera away.
- **Move quietly.** Inside, families may be kneeling among candles and petals on the floor. Step around offerings, never over them, and keep your voice low.

## Pascual Abaj

On a hilltop outside the town centre stands Pascual Abaj, a shrine where, in Visit Centroamérica's words, "ancestral customs are practiced before an idol of stone called Pascual Abaj", with offerings that can range from candles to burning incense ([Visit Centroamérica](https://www.visitcentroamerica.com/en/visitar/santo-tomas-church-pascual-abaj-shrine-and-chichicastenango-market/)).

You may arrive to find a ceremony under way, led by a spiritual guide for a family or community. If so, keep your distance, stay quiet and wait to be acknowledged before approaching. Do not step into the ceremonial area, touch the altar or offerings, or photograph without asking. Many visitors go with a local guide, who can explain what is happening and whether it is appropriate to watch. Do not walk up alone; go with a guide or in company, and ask in town about the current situation on the path.

## Photography and respect

The UK government's travel advice is direct: "Do not take photographs without permission, especially of children." It adds that this is particularly important in more remote areas such as Quiché, the department Chichicastenango is in, and that there have been attacks linked to fears about child kidnapping ([UK FCDO safety and security, checked November 2026](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/guatemala/safety-and-security)). Ask first, accept a no gracefully, and remember that a stallholder who has just sold you something is not obliged to pose.

Wide shots of the market and the plaza, without singling people out, are usually less intrusive. If someone agrees to a portrait, show them the picture afterwards and say thank you.

## Textiles: huipiles and how to shop well

The market is famous for its textiles. Visit Centroamérica lists blouses, tablecloths, bags, trousers and belts, hand-embroidered or loom-woven, alongside ceramics and masks, some used in traditional dances ([Visit Centroamérica](https://www.visitcentroamerica.com/en/visitar/santo-tomas-church-pascual-abaj-shrine-and-chichicastenango-market/)). The centrepiece for many visitors is the huipil, the woven blouse worn by Maya women. Designs, colours and motifs vary from community to community, so a huipil can tell you a great deal about where its maker is from.

### Telling handwoven from machine-made

Backstrap-loom weaving is slow, skilled work. Handwoven pieces often show slight irregularities, a dense hand and motifs that look the same on both sides or are clearly worked into the cloth, while machine-made pieces tend to be perfectly uniform. Ask the seller who wove it, where and how. Most are glad to explain, and the conversation is part of the pleasure.

### Bargaining fairly

Bargaining is normal in the market, but it is a conversation, not a contest.

- **Have a figure in mind** for what the piece is worth to you before you ask the price.
- **Counter politely and settle somewhere in the middle.** Do not haggle hard over a sum that matters far more to the seller than to you.
- **Pay more for time and skill.** A huipil that took weeks on a loom should not be bargained like a printed bag.
- **Only bargain if you mean to buy.** Walking away once a price is agreed is poor form.
- **Carry small notes**, and keep valuables out of sight in the crowds; the UK government advises against displaying valuables like cameras and phones ([UK FCDO safety and security](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/guatemala/safety-and-security)).

Buying directly from weavers, or from stalls run by the families who make what they sell, keeps more of your money in the community.

## What to eat

The food side of the market is as much a part of the day as the textiles. Head for the covered market area and the comedores, simple eateries with shared tables where cooks work over big pots and griddles. What is on offer changes with the day and the season, but you might find:

- **Atol**, a warm, thick drink often made with maize, served in the morning. Variations may use rice or other grains.
- **Tamales and chuchitos**, maize dough steamed in leaves with a filling or sauce.
- **Caldos and stews**, hearty soups served with tortillas.
- **Fresh tortillas**, patted out by hand and cooked on the griddle.
- **Fruit and vegetables** from the surrounding highlands, piled high on the produce stalls.

Point, smile and ask what something is. Busy stalls with a steady local crowd are a good sign. Choose food that is cooked hot in front of you, and drink bottled or treated water.

## Getting there

Most travellers visit Chichicastenango as a day trip from Antigua or Lake Atitlán. From the lake it is a winding drive up into the highlands; from Antigua it is a longer journey, most of a morning on the road, so an early start pays off. Shared shuttles run on market days and private transfers are widely available. The UK government's regional advice on routes around Sololá, Panajachel and Lake Atitlán is worth reading if you are coming from the lake ([UK FCDO regional risks, checked November 2026](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/guatemala/regional-risks)), and it suggests contacting PROATUR, Guatemala's tourist assistance service, for route advice.

Staying overnight is also an option, and lets you see the market set up in the early morning before the day-trippers arrive. Travel advice changes, so re-check the UK FCDO and the US State Department ([travel.state.gov](https://travel.state.gov/)) before you go. ## A good market day

Arrive early, wander slowly, eat where the locals eat, buy something made by the person selling it, and leave space for the parts of Chichi that are not for sale: the prayers on the steps, the smoke at Pascual Abaj, the quiet inside the church.

If you would like someone to handle the early start, the drive and the introductions, a local operator can arrange a market-day trip with a guide who knows the community's customs, which makes it far easier to visit Santo Tomás and Pascual Abaj respectfully and to find weavers selling their own work.
