It is dark, and it is properly cold. You are sitting on a log outside your tent with every layer you own zipped up to the chin, hands wrapped around a cup of something hot, legs aching from the climb. Across a gulf of black air, Fuego clears its throat. First a deep boom you feel in your chest, then a fountain of orange sparks thrown up against the stars, then glowing rocks tumbling and bouncing down its flank long after the sound has rolled past. Nobody at camp says much. Somebody laughs, quietly, out of sheer disbelief. A few minutes later it happens again.

That is the reason people climb Acatenango, and it is unforgettable. It is also a serious mountain beside an active volcano, and it pays to understand what you are taking on.

## What the overnight hike is like

Most trips leave from Antigua early in the morning and drive to a trailhead on the lower slopes. From there the walk is steady and steep: farmland first, then forest, then cloud forest where the air cools and the path turns to loose volcanic grit that slides back under every step. Expect a long, hard day of climbing, most of it uphill, with regular breaks.

Camp sits high on Acatenango, facing Fuego across the saddle between them. You arrive tired, often in cloud, and the temperature falls fast once the sun goes. Then the evening becomes about waiting and watching. On a clear night Fuego's eruptions are the show; on a cloudy one you may hear more than you see, and that is part of the gamble.

Many groups get up before dawn for an optional push to Acatenango's summit for sunrise, with views across to other volcanoes on a clear morning. Then it is a knee-jarring descent back to the trailhead, and a hot shower in Antigua has rarely felt so good.

## Why you go with a licensed guide

This is not a hike to do independently. Guatemala's disaster agency CONRED, in guidance published on 11 November 2026, advises hikers to use an authorised guide and avoid unknown routes or walking alone ([CONRED](https://conred.gob.gt/asciende-volcanes-con-seguridad-y-prevencion/), checked November 2026).

The UK government is blunt about the risks. Its travel advice says: "Local tour organisers tend to underestimate the risks and deaths have occurred. There is no mountain rescue service." ([UK FCDO travel advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/guatemala/safety-and-security), checked November 2026). That makes it worth choosing your guide carefully: someone licensed, experienced on this mountain, who turns back when conditions demand it and who watches the official volcano reports rather than the weather app alone.

A good guide also brings the mountain to life: the trail, the forest, the weather and the way Fuego behaves from one night to the next.

If you are travelling on a US passport, read the US State Department's current Guatemala travel advisory on its official website as well, and check it again shortly before you travel.

## Fitness, altitude and cold

### Fitness

You do not need to be an athlete, but you do need to be comfortably fit. If you can walk uphill for several hours with a light pack and still enjoy it, you are in good shape. If you rarely walk far, start training weeks ahead with long hilly walks and stairs. If your trip includes a porter to carry heavier kit, it makes a real difference. Be honest with yourself and your guide: there is no shame in turning back.

### Altitude

Altitude can affect anyone on a climb like this, however fit. The NHS says you can get altitude sickness at high altitude, "usually more than 2,500 metres above sea level", and that you are more likely to get it if you climb quickly ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/altitude-sickness/), checked November 2026). Symptoms include headache, loss of appetite, nausea, tiredness, dizziness and difficulty sleeping. If symptoms get worse or do not improve, the NHS advises going lower, and it lists serious warning signs, such as confusion, breathlessness at rest or a frothy cough, that mean getting medical help and descending straight away.

Practically, that means spending a few days in Antigua before you climb, drinking plenty of water, skipping alcohol the night before and at camp, and telling your guide early if you feel unwell. CONRED's own advice is to stop and ask for help if you feel exhausted, dizzy, nauseous or unwell on the trail ([CONRED](https://conred.gob.gt/asciende-volcanes-con-seguridad-y-prevencion/)). If you have a heart or lung condition, or have had altitude sickness before, talk to your doctor or a travel clinic before booking.

### Cold

People underestimate this more than anything else. The UK government notes that "Some volcanoes are at high altitude with freezing temperatures at night. Warm clothing and waterproofs are essential." ([UK FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/guatemala/safety-and-security)). You will climb in a T-shirt and sit at camp in a down jacket, hat and gloves, and still feel the chill. Wind and rain can make it much worse.

## What to pack

CONRED recommends enough water and food, a torch, a first-aid kit, warm waterproof clothing and sturdy footwear for uneven ground ([CONRED](https://conred.gob.gt/asciende-volcanes-con-seguridad-y-prevencion/)). Check with your guide what is supplied, then build around it:

- Sturdy hiking boots or shoes with good grip, already broken in.
- Layers: a breathable base layer, a fleece, a warm insulated jacket, and a waterproof shell.
- Warm hat, gloves and a buff or scarf for the wind.
- Thermal leggings and warm socks for sleeping.
- A headtorch with fresh batteries.
- Plenty of water and snacks you actually like eating when tired.
- Sun cream, sunglasses and a sun hat for the climb.
- A small first-aid kit, blister plasters and any personal medicines.
- A rain cover or bin liner for your pack.
- A bag to carry all your rubbish back down.

CONRED also asks hikers to leave no rubbish, avoid lighting fires and respect protected natural areas. Leave camp cleaner than you found it.

## When the volcano says no

Fuego is watched constantly. INSIVUMEH, Guatemala's national institute for seismology, volcanology, meteorology and hydrology, monitors it continuously and publishes [daily volcano bulletins](https://insivumeh.gob.gt/vulcanologia-boletines-diarios-1/) and special bulletins when activity changes.

That activity can shut the trail with little notice. On 3 August 2026, INSIVUMEH confirmed a new eruptive phase at Fuego, with pyroclastic density currents down its western flank and an ash column reaching up to 5,000 metres above sea level ([INSIVUMEH](https://insivumeh.gob.gt/insivumeh-mantiene-monitoreo-permanente-ante-fase-eruptiva-del-volcan-de-fuego/)). The next day CONRED reported a police presence at the Acatenango entrance to stop tourists climbing, explaining that the restriction responded to the danger Fuego's activity posed to hikers, tourists and guides ([CONRED](https://conred.gob.gt/sistema-conred-resguarda-ingreso-al-volcan-acatenango/)).

Some tours offer an extra trek from camp along the ridge towards Fuego itself. Be very cautious. On 9 August 2026, the government news agency AGN reported CONRED's statement that nobody was authorised to climb Fuego, and that the restriction would stay while the risk conditions remained ([AGN](https://agn.gt/ascenso-al-volcan-de-fuego-permanece-restringido/)). Check whether that still applies before you agree to anything.

Fuego's power is not abstract. CONRED's records describe the families affected by the eruption of 3 June 2018 and the forensic exhumations later carried out at San Miguel Los Lotes ([CONRED](https://conred.gob.gt/nota-de-prensa-no-2110-exhumaciones-en-san-miguel-los-lotes/)). The people living on these slopes carry that memory; it is worth carrying it with you too.

So, before you go:

1. Check the latest INSIVUMEH bulletin and CONRED's announcements the day before and the morning of your hike.
2. Ask your guide or operator directly whether the route is open and what the plan is if it closes.
3. Build a spare day into your Antigua stay so a closure or bad weather does not cost you the experience.
4. Follow any instruction from the authorities on the mountain without argument.

The UK government's advice is to check INSIVUMEH and CONRED for access, restrictions and recommendations before climbing, and to follow local authorities' advice ([UK FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/guatemala/safety-and-security), checked November 2026). In an emergency, CONRED lists 119 as its number ([CONRED](https://conred.gob.gt/asciende-volcanes-con-seguridad-y-prevencion/)). Advice and access change, so re-check all of this shortly before you travel. ## Fitting it into your trip

Give yourself time in [Antigua](/posts/antigua-what-to-see-how-long-to-stay/) to acclimatise first, and leave a lazy day afterwards; your legs will thank you. The [ten-day Guatemala itinerary](/posts/ten-day-guatemala-itinerary/) shows how the hike slots in before heading on to the lake.

The night on Acatenango is one of those experiences people talk about for years, and the difference between a great trip and a miserable one usually comes down to preparation and the people you go with. A local operator who knows the guides, watches the volcano reports and can move your climbing day when Fuego or the weather says no is worth having on your side.
