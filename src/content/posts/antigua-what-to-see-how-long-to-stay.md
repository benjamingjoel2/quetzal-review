You step out of a doorway into thin morning light. The cobbles are still damp, a woman is sweeping the pavement outside a shuttered shop, and at the end of the street a yellow arch frames a volcano so large and so close it looks painted on. Somewhere a church bell starts up, then another answers it. A pick-up rattles past with a load of flowers. You have not had coffee yet, and already Antigua has made its case.

This is the town most travellers meet first in Guatemala, and many end up wishing they had given it longer. Here is what makes it matter, what to see, what to eat, and how many nights to plan for.

## Why Antigua matters

Antigua was not always "old". It was once Santiago de los Caballeros de Guatemala, the capital of a colonial kingdom that, according to the World Bank's account of the city's history, governed most of Central America as far as southern Mexico ([World Bank Blogs](https://blogs.worldbank.org/es/latinamerica/historia-desastres-y-resiliencia-la-cronica-de-antigua-guatemala)). The Guatemalan government news agency AGN describes it as the kingdom's third capital, founded in 1542–3 in the Panchoy valley after earlier sites were abandoned ([AGN](https://agn.gt/antigua-guatemala-a-251-anos-de-los-terremotos-de-santa-marta)).

Then the ground moved. The Santa Marta earthquakes culminated in the quake of 29 July 1773, which brought down temples, palaces and grand houses, most of them Baroque ([AGN](https://agn.gt/antigua-guatemala-a-251-anos-de-los-terremotos-de-santa-marta)). The capital was moved for good to what is now Guatemala City, but the survivors slowly rebuilt and began calling their town Antigua Guatemala. The World Bank piece explains that the city's partial abandonment in 1776, and later rules restricting new building, preserved both the ruins and the 16th-century grid of streets, and that this is why UNESCO listed it as a World Heritage site in 1979 ([World Bank Blogs](https://blogs.worldbank.org/es/latinamerica/historia-desastres-y-resiliencia-la-cronica-de-antigua-guatemala)).

That history is what you are walking through. The squat, thick-walled churches are part of a regional style the same source calls "Barroco Antigüeño", built to resist the tremors that are a fact of life here. The roofless naves and fallen domes are the earthquakes' signature. Antigua is not a museum, though. People live, work, worship and go to school inside this setting, so walk it with the same courtesy you would want on your own street.

## What to see

### Santa Catalina arch

The arch is the postcard, and it earns it. It began as a practical solution: the nuns of the Convent of Santa Catalina Mártir lived in enclosure and could not go out into the street, so when they needed the property opposite, the authorities allowed a bridge, built in 1693, with a covered corridor the nuns could cross unseen ([AGN](https://agn.gt/el-arco-de-santa-catalina-icono-de-antigua-guatemala/)). The little clock tower came later, after the 1773 earthquakes. Come early, before the crowds, when the light is soft and Volcán de Agua sits squarely in the frame.

### The cathedral and its ruins

On the east side of the central park, the cathedral's restored front hides a different world behind it: a vast roofless space of broken arches and pillars open to the sky. Stand in the middle and the scale of what the city lost becomes real. The park itself, shaded and busy, is the best place in town to sit and watch Antigua go about its day.

### La Merced

La Merced is the church people photograph almost as much as the arch: a richly decorated yellow-and-white facade covered in plasterwork, the kind of Baroque that rewards a slow look. Inside and around it there is real devotional life, so dress respectfully and keep your voice low during services.

### Convents and ruins

Antigua is scattered with ruined convents and monasteries in different states of repair, some open to visit, some glimpsed only through a gate. Wandering between them is the point: cloisters with fountains, moss creeping up stone, a bell tower standing alone. Check locally which are open on the day you visit, as access changes.

### Cerro de la Cruz

For the view that explains the whole town, climb up to Cerro de la Cruz, a hillside viewpoint just north of the centre. From the cross you look down on the grid of rooftops and domes with Volcán de Agua rising beyond it. Go with others and in daylight, and ask your hotel about the current situation on the path before you set out.

### Markets

The main market, near the bus terminal on the west side of town, is loud, crowded and full of life: piles of fruit, sacks of dried chillies, stalls of cut flowers, cooks working over griddles. It is a working market, not a show, so buy something, be patient, and always ask before you photograph anyone. Handicraft markets are easy to find too; take your time and favour pieces where you can talk to the maker.

## Semana Santa and the alfombras

If your dates are flexible, think about Holy Week. Semana Santa in Guatemala was inscribed by UNESCO in 2022 on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. UN News describes processions, vigils and funeral marches, seasonal food, decorated house fronts, and carpets, orchards and altars created for the occasion, traditions passed between generations for centuries ([UN News](https://news.un.org/en/story/2022/11/1131212)).

In Antigua those carpets are the alfombras: intricate designs laid by hand across the street, only to be walked over by the procession and swept away. Watching a family work on one, then seeing the heavy andas sway slowly over it in a haze of incense, is one of the most moving sights in the country. Remember that this is a religious observance first. Do not step on an alfombra, give the processions room, and ask before photographing people.

Holy Week is also the busiest time of year in town. Dates change annually, so check the calendar early and book well ahead.

## What to eat

### Pepián

Start with pepián, a deep, smoky stew thickened with toasted seeds and chillies, usually with chicken, vegetables and rice, and eaten with tortillas. AGN notes that it can be red or black, that it turns up at weddings, birthdays and religious celebrations, and that the Ministry of Culture and Sports declared it part of the nation's intangible cultural heritage by ministerial agreement 801-2007 ([AGN](https://agn.gt/preparan-segundo-festival-departamental-del-pepian/)). A good bowl is warming, complex and quietly addictive.

### Chocolate

Chocolate is everywhere in Antigua, from steaming cups of hot chocolate to bars made in small workshops. A hands-on class, where you roast and grind cacao and mix your own, is a fun way to spend a rainy afternoon.

### Coffee

The volcanic slopes around the valley are coffee country, and the town is full of places pouring beans grown on nearby fincas. Order something simple and taste it properly, then go and see where it comes from (see day trips, below).

### Street food

In the evenings, look around the park and the streets near the churches for griddles and carts: tostadas piled with guacamole or beans, sweet and savoury snacks, grilled corn. Choose busy stalls where food is cooked fresh in front of you, and you will eat very well.

## Day trips

**A coffee farm.** A morning on a working finca shows you the full journey, from cherry on the bush to cup, and the views across to the volcanoes are a bonus. Ask about the people who pick and process the coffee; it is a real livelihood, not a theme park.

**Pacaya.** An active volcano that is a popular half-day hike with a guide. Volcanic conditions change, so go with a licensed guide and follow local instructions.

**Acatenango.** The big one: an overnight hike to camp facing Fuego and watch it erupt after dark. It is demanding and depends on volcanic activity, so read our guide to [hiking Acatenango to watch Fuego erupt](/posts/hiking-acatenango-to-watch-fuego-erupt/) before you commit.

**Villages around the valley.** Nearby towns offer weaving, markets and a slower pace of life. Go with a local guide who can introduce you properly, buy directly from weavers where you can, and always ask before taking photographs.

## How long to stay

This is advice rather than a rule, but most travellers are happiest with two to four nights.

**Two nights** is the minimum to enjoy it without rushing. One full day lets you walk the centre, see the arch, the cathedral ruins, La Merced and a convent or two, climb Cerro de la Cruz and eat well. You will leave wanting more.

**Three nights** is the sweet spot for most first-time visitors. Add a coffee farm or a village morning and a cooking class, and you still have time to sit in the park and do nothing.

**Four nights or more** suits hikers, food lovers and anyone arriving tired from a long flight. Acatenango alone takes two days and leaves you wanting a lazy day afterwards, and Antigua is a comfortable base for recovering before you head to the lake. Read our [first-timer's guide to Guatemala](/posts/first-timers-guide-to-guatemala/) and the [ten-day itinerary](/posts/ten-day-guatemala-itinerary/) to see how Antigua fits with everything else.

Families and slower travellers should lean towards the longer end, and anyone visiting during Semana Santa should stay for the whole of Holy Week if they can. If you are pressed for time, it is better to give Antigua two unhurried nights than to cram it into one before moving on to [Lake Atitlán](/posts/lake-atitlan-which-village-to-stay-in/).

Antigua rewards travellers who leave room in the day for wandering, so the easiest way to enjoy it is to let someone local take care of the logistics: where to stay, which convent to see in the best light, which farm to visit, and whether the volcano is safe to climb this week. A local operator can shape those days around your pace and leave you free to follow the bells.
